\section{Introduction}

In this project, we will investigate the use of Monte Carlo simulation (MCS)  to estimate the exceedance probability $\mathbb{P}(L > x)$ of a loss $$ L = \delta^T\Delta S + \Delta S^T\Gamma\Delta S,$$
where, $delta \in \mathbb{R}^m$, $\Gamma \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $\Delta S \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_S)$ is a (multivariate) normal random variable. The exceedance probability is the probability that the loss $L$ exceeds a certain threshold $x = 10$. \vspace{.5cm}


In the parts $(a)$, $(b)$ and $(c)$, we will focus on the one-dimensional case, $m= 1$. These three parts will compare crude MCS with MCS where we introduce control variates and importance samplingm respectively. In part $(d)$, the differences of these methods are visuallized by comparison of the confidence intervals of the estimates that are obtained from each method. \vspace{.5cm}

Parameters for the various codes snippets can be seen in the appendix in \autoref{code_params}.

\section{Crude Monte Carlo Simulation (a)}
In this section, we will implement a crude Monte Carlo simulation to estimate the exceedance probability $\mathbb{P}(L > x)$ for the one-dimensional case ($m=1$). We are interested in ${P}(L > x)$. We may introduce the indicator function $\mathbb{I}(L > x)$ of the event $L > x$. If we compute the expectation of the indicator function, we obtain the exceedance probability

\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(L > x) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}(L > x)] = 0\cdot\mathbb{P}(L > x) + 1\cdot\mathbb{P}(L > x) = \mathbb{P}(L > x),
\end{equation}
by the bernoulli property of the indicator function. We estimate the exceedance probability by use of the following Monte Carlo estimator
\begin{equation}
    \hat\ell = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}(L_i > x) \xrightarrow[N \to \infty]{a.s} \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}(L > x)],
\end{equation}
by the law of large numbers. The crude Monte Carlo simulation is implemented in the code snippet seen in \autoref{code_a}. A estimate of
\begin{equation*}
    \hat{\ell} \approx 0.0103
\end{equation*}
is found with a variance of $s^2 = 1.03\cdot 10^{-7}$.

\section{Control Variable (b)}
A control variable is now introduced with the aim of reducing the variance of the Monte Carlo estimator. The estimator is now given by
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{CV} = Y - \beta(Z - \mathbb{E}[Z]),
\end{equation}
where $Y = \mathbb{I}(L > x)$ is the original estimator, $Z$ is the control variable and $\beta$ is a constant. It follows from proposition 5.1, that the optimal value of $\beta$ is given by
\begin{equation*}
    \beta^* = \frac{\text{Cov}(Y, Z)}{\text{Var}(Z)}.
\end{equation*}
When considering the choice of a control variable, we should aim to choose a variable that satisfies the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Closed form expression for the expectation of the control variable $\mathbb{E}[Z]$.
    \item The control variable should be as correlated with the original estimator $Y$ as possible.
    \item The reduction in variance should be significant enough to justify the additional computational cost of calculating the control variable.
\end{enumerate}
Given $L$, two choices of control variables are natural. The first one is the linear term of L given by:
\begin{equation*}
    Z_1 = \delta^T\Delta S, 
\end{equation*}
and the second one is the quadratic term of L given by:
\begin{equation*}
    Z_2 = \Delta S^T\Gamma\Delta S.
\end{equation*}
Since $m, \, \Gamma = 1$, and the fact that $Delta_S$ is standard normal, $Z_1 \sim \delta\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Z_2 \sim \chi^2(1)$, and as such their expectations are given by
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb{E}[Z_1] = 0, \quad \mathbb{E}[Z_2] = 1.
\end{equation*}
To test which control variable is the best, a small pilot study is conducted to find the sample correlation between the original estimator $Y$ and the two control variables $Z_1$ and $Z_2$, yeilding
\begin{equation*}
    \text{Corr}(Y, Z_1) \approx 0.25, \quad \text{Corr}(Y, Z_2) \approx 0.37.
\end{equation*}
Given the results of the pilot study, and the fact that $Z_2$ is not much more computationally expensive to compute than $Z_1$, $Z_2$ is chosen as a control variable. This leads to a reduction in variance of approximately $26\%$ compared to the crude MCS. Since crude MCS has a $\sqrt{N}$ convergence rate, and we we roughly double the number of operations by computing the control variable, it seem reasonable to stick with crude MCS and double the number of samples, in order to achieve a reduction in variance of the order $\sqrt{2}$.

\section{Importance-Sampling (c)}
Since we are interested in estimating a relatively rare event, we can use importance sampling to reduce the variance. We are interested in designing a new distribution under which the event $L > x$ is likely to occur. For numerical stability, it is desirable to choose a distribution that is not too far from the original distribution. If we inspect $L$, it is compromised of a linear and a quadratic term. We may consider if we want to shift the mean of $\Delta_S$ upwards or downwards. Since we have a quadratic term, that is always positive, shifting the mean in a negative direction, will be somewhat dampened by the linear term, as such we would require larger shifting in the negative direction to observe the same effect, therefore, a positive shift is chosen. \vspace{.5cm}
The expexctation of the loss $L$ is given by
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb{E}[L] = \mathbb{E}[\delta^T\Delta S + \Delta S^T\Gamma\Delta S] = \mathbb{E}[\delta^T\Delta S] + \mathbb{E}[\Delta S^T\Gamma\Delta S] = 0 + 1 = 1,
\end{equation*}
Now we would like to shift the mean of $\Delta S$ such that the expectation of $L$ is equal to the threshold $x = 10$, such that the rare event $L > x$, is no longer rare. We do this by setting the expectation of $L$ under the new distribution $\mathbb{Q}$ to be equal to $x = 10$:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L] = \mathbb{E}[\delta^T(\Delta S + \mu) + (\Delta S + \mu)^T\Gamma(\Delta S + \mu)] = 10.
\end{equation*}
as adding a mean $\mu$ to $\Delta S$, will shift its mean by $\mu$, so for $m = 1$
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L] = 2\mu + \mathbb{E}(\Delta S^2) + \mu^2 = 2\mu + 1 + \mu^2 = 10 \implies \mu(\mu + 2) = 9.
\end{equation*}
The positive solution to this equation is $\sqrt(10) -1 \approx 2.16$, and in accordance with above reasoning, we see that a negative shift would require $\mu \approx -4.16$, which is considerably larger in magnitude. 

\vspace{.5cm}
Using this design choice, variance reduction on the order of $3000\%$ is achieved. As such, importance-sampling offers a significant improvement compared to crude MCS, far outweighing the additional computational cost of computing the likelihood ratios. \section{Closed form expressison of $\mathbb{P}(L > x)$ for the given parameters (e)}

We wish to show that the exceedance probability can be computed in a closed form expression involving the CDF $\phi(x)$ of the standard normal distribution. Given the parameters, $m = 1$, $\delta = 2$, $\Gamma = 1$, $\Sigma_S = 1$ and $x = 10$, we have $\Delta_S \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $L = 2\Delta S + \Delta S^2$. We can rewrite the exceedance probability as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(L > x) = \mathbb{P}(\Delta S^2 + 2\Delta S - 10 > 0) 
\end{equation}
The left hand side is a quadratic polynomial in $\Delta S$, we note that it is convex, as such, this probability is equivalent to the probablity that $\Delta S $ is lesser than the smaller root of the polynomial or greater than the larger root of the polynomial. The roots of the polynomial are given by
\begin{equation*}
    r_{-} = -1 - \sqrt{11}, \quad r_{+} = -1 + \sqrt{11}.
\end{equation*}
We note that these events are disjoint, so we can write the exceedance probability as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(L > x) = \mathbb{P}( \Delta S < r_{-} \cup \Delta S > r_{+}) = \mathbb{P}(\Delta S < r_{-}) + \mathbb{P}(\Delta S > r_{+})
\end{equation}
Since $\Delta S \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, we can express the exceedance probability in terms of the CDF $\phi(x)$ of the standard normal distribution as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}(L > x) = \phi(r_{-}) + 1 - \phi(r_{+})  \approx 0.0103,
\end{equation}
which is consistent with the results obtained from the Monte Carlo simulations.
\subsection{Is this true for other distributions?}
We imposed or used no special restrictions on the distribution of $\Delta S$, as such, this method could be used to compute the exceedance probability under any distribution, as long as the CDF of the distribution is available.
\subsection{Generalization of crude and control- MCS to other distributions}
As long as we can sample from the distribution of $\Delta S$, we can use crude MCS to estimate the exceedance probability without any modifications. \vspace{0.5cm}

For control variable MCS, we do have the requirement that the expectation of the control variable is known in closed form - however this is true in general, and changing the sampling distribution of $\Delta S$ does not change this fact. As such, the control variable MCS can be generalized to other distributions without any modifications other than that the control variable $Z = h(\Delta S)$ has to be chosen such that its expectation $\mathbb{E}[Z]$ is known in closed form.

\section{Cholesky Decomposition (f)}
We wish to find a matrix $\tilde{C}$ such that $\Delta_S = \tilde{C}Z$, where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2)$, it follows from definition 3.8 that we can find such a matrix $\tilde{C}$ by performing a Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix $\Sigma_S$. We define the upper triangular matrix     $\tilde{C}$ as
\begin{equation*}
    \tilde{C} = \begin{bmatrix}
        a & 0\\
        b & c
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation*}
with unknowns $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$, and solve
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{C}\tilde{C}^T = \begin{bmatrix}
        a & 0\\
        b & c
    \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
        a & b\\
        0 & c
    \end{bmatrix}  = \Sigma_S = \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & \frac{1}{2}\\
        \frac{1}{2} & 1
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This produces the following system of equations
\begin{align*}
    a^2  &= 1\\
    ab &= \frac{1}{2}\\
    b^2 + c^2 &= 1.
\end{align*}
$a^2 = 1 \implies a = 1$, and thus $b = \frac{1}{2}$. We can than solve $\frac{1}{4} - c^2 = 1 \implies c = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}$.
\begin{equation*}
    \tilde{C} = \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & \frac{1}{2}\\
        0 & \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
\subsection{Eigen decomposition of $\tilde{C}^T\tilde{C}$}

First we compute $\tilde{C}^T\tilde{C}$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{C}^T\tilde{C} = \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0\\
        \frac{1}{2} & \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}
    \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & \frac{1}{2}\\
        0 & \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}
    \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{5}{4} & \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}\\
        \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}} & \frac{3}{4}
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We note that $\tilde{C}^T\tilde{C}$ is a real symmetric matrix, it follows from the spectral theorem that there exists and orthogonal matrix $U$ and a diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{C}^T\tilde{C} = U\Lambda U^T.
    \label{eq:eigendecomp}
\end{equation}
where the columns of $U$ are the eigenvectors of $\tilde{C}^T\tilde{C}$ and the diagonal entries of $\Lambda$ are the corresponding eigenvalues. Thus, we need to solve the eigenvalue problem

\begin{equation}
    \text{det}(\tilde{C}^T\tilde{C} - \lambda I) = 0,
\end{equation}

which yields the characteristic polynomial

\begin{equation*}
    \left(\frac{5}{4} - \lambda\right)\left(\frac{3}{4} - \lambda\right) - \left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}\right)^2 = 0.
\end{equation*}

This yeilds the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = \dfrac{1}{2}, \; \lambda_2 = \dfrac{3}{2}$, now we find the eigenvectors by solving the system of equations
\begin{equation*}
    (\tilde{C}^T\tilde{C} - \lambda I)u = 0,
\end{equation*}
which yields the eigenvectors
\begin{equation*}
    u_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}} \\ \frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}, \quad u_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{1}{2} \\ \sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}  \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
and $U$ is given as the matrix with columns $u_1$ and $u_2$, and $\Lambda$ is the diagonal matrix with entries $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$.

The computation is carried out in the code snippet seen in \autoref{code_eigen_decomposition}.

\subsection{New expression of L}
Using our Cholesky decomposition, we have $\Delta S = \tilde{C}Z$, where $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2)$. It then follows that we can rewrite $L$ as
\begin{equation}
    L = \delta^T\tilde{C}Z + Z^T\tilde{C}^T\Gamma\tilde{C}Z.
\end{equation}
Now since $\Gamma = I_2$, this simplifies to
\begin{equation}
    L = \delta^T\tilde{C}Z + Z^T\tilde{C}^T\tilde{C}Z.
\end{equation}
We define $Z = [Z_1, Z_2]^T$, and use the eigen decomposition \eqref{eq:eigendecomp} to rewrite $L$ as
\begin{equation}
    L = \delta^T\tilde{C}Z + Z^TU\Lambda U^TZ = \delta^T\tilde{C}Z + (U^TZ)^T\Lambda U^TZ .
    \label{eq:Lnew}
\end{equation}
Not let $C = \tilde{C}U \implies C^T = U^T\tilde{C}^T$, and $b = C^T\delta = U^T\tilde{C}^T\delta$. If we look at the first term of $L$, and use the fact that $U$ is orthogonal, we have
\begin{equation}
    \delta^T\tilde{C}Z = (UU^T\tilde{C}^T\delta)^TZ = (Ub)^TZ = b^TU^TZ.
    \label{eq:bterm}
\end{equation}
Now it is useful to note that the linear transformation $U^TZ \sim \mathcal{N}(0, U^TU)$, orthogonality then gives that
$U^TZ \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_2) = Z$. As such \eqref{eq:bterm} simplifies to
\begin{equation}
    \delta^T\tilde{C}Z = b^TU^TZ = b^TZ.
\end{equation}


Using this property, again we can rewrite \eqref{eq:Lnew} as
\begin{equation}
    L = b^TZ + (U^TZ)^T \Lambda U^TZ = b^TZ + Z^T\Lambda Z,
\end{equation}
using \eqref{eq:bterm}. The first term is a inner product of two vectors, and the since $\Lambda$ is diagonal, the second term is a weighted sum of squares of the entries of $Z$ - as such we arrive at the desired expression of $L$:
\begin{equation}
    L = b_1Z_1 + b_2Z_2 + \lambda_1Z_1^2 + \lambda_2Z_2^2.
    \label{eq:Lfinal}
\end{equation}

\section{Exponential tilting (g)}
We are now interested in importance sampling similar to (c), but for the new parameters. Recall that in extension to exercise 27, we discussed that exponential tilting for exponential families, retains distributional form under a new shifted parameter.
\begin{equation}
    \int_A \prod_{i\leq 2} f(z) e^{\theta_i^Tz - \psi^*(\theta_i)} dz, \label{eq:tilt}
\end{equation}
Our original distribution is $\Delta S \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_S)$, and we can write the density of $\Delta S$ as

\begin{equation*}
    f(\delta S) = \dfrac{1}{2\pi}|\Sigma_S|^{-\frac{1}{2}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\delta S)^T\Sigma_S^{-1}(\delta S)}.
\end{equation*}
where $|\Sigma_S| = \frac{3}{4}$.


\begin{equation*}
    f(z) e^{\theta_i^Tz - \psi^*(\theta_i)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}z^2} e^{\theta_i z - \frac{1}{2}\theta_i^2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(z - \theta_i)^2}.
\end{equation*}
We see that the new importance sampling distribution from \eqref{eq:tilt} is a joint normal distribution$ (Q_1, Q_2)$, where $ Q_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_i, 1)$, and $Q_1 \perp Q_2$. As such the likelihood ratio is given by
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{f(z)}{g(z)} = \dfrac{\prod_{i\leq 2} f(z_i)}{\prod_{i\leq 2} g(z_i)} = \prod_{i\leq 2} e^{-\theta_i z_i + \frac{1}{2}\theta_i^2}.
\end{equation}
